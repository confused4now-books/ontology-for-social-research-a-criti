# Chapter 1: Introduction to Ontological Analysis in Social Research

## Introduction[^1]

Consider a situation that will be familiar to anyone who has spent time in the literature of any highly discussed field. Imagine two research teams investigate the same phenomenon, say for example, the persistence of educational inequality, and both seemingly do everything right. Both draw on adequate samples, employ validated instruments, apply methods appropriate to their data, and situate their findings within established theoretical traditions. One concludes that inequality is reproduced primarily through the unequal distribution of material resources: underfunded schools, precarious household incomes, differential access to the infrastructures of educational success. The other concludes that cultural frameworks and meaning-making practices are decisive: that the language, expectations, and classificatory schemes children carry into classrooms are what determine outcomes, largely independently of resource levels. Both findings are well-supported. Both teams can defend every methodological decision. And yet their conclusions appear to be in direct contradiction.

The standard response to this situation, form both sides, would be argumentative. Each team searches for the flaw in the other's design, perhaps a sampling bias, a confounded variable, or simply dismisses the methods as unsuitable for gaining scientific insight on the situation. The quantitative researcher charges the qualitative study with subjectivity, insufficient generalisability, and the undue influence of the researcher's own interpretive framework. In contrast, the qualitative researcher charges the quantitative study with reductionism, with flattening the complexity of lived experience into variables that bear no resemblance to the social processes they purport to measure. Both critiques carry at least one grain of truth, but neither resolves the disagreement. And so it goes along, the field accumulates contradictory findings, researchers retreat into the assurance that their own methods followed established norms and therefore carry analytical merit, and the deeper question of "why do equally rigorous studies produce incompatible conclusions?" goes largely unasked. Yet, the replication crisis across the social sciences has made this problem impossible to ignore: a substantial proportion of published findings, produced by competent researchers following field-standard procedures, nonetheless fail to be reproduced under equivalent conditions. Attempts at more transparent data, or methodological refinement alone have not resolved this, and we would argue that this is because the problem does not originate at the methodological level.

This book proposes that many of the most persistent disagreements in social research, especially concerning methods, are not errors to be corrected but the surface manifestation of deeper differences in how researchers understand the nature of social reality. When one study finds that material conditions are causally primary and another finds that meanings and interpretations are, the disagreement is unlikely to be settled by better sampling or more sophisticated analysis. It reflects a prior and largely unexamined question: what kind of thing is social reality, and what does it therefore require of any investigation that claims to understand it? These are *ontological* questions, and the argument developed throughout this book is that making them explicit is not a philosophical luxury reserved for theorists but a practical necessity for any researcher who wants to understand not only what their methods, and thus findings show, but why those findings look the way they do and what they are incapable of showing.

However, responding to this challenge requires a different starting point from that offered by most traditional research methods textbooks. Rather than beginning with the research cycle or data collection this book starts with fundamental philosophical questions about the nature of social reality. This approach reflects a conviction that methodological choices (decisions about what to study, how to study it, and what would count as adequate evidence) cannot be separated from deeper ontological commitments about what exists in the social world and how different aspects of social reality relate to each other. After all, what you think is there will influence how you think you can find out more about it. Nonetheless, traditional methods texts often treat these philosophical foundations as either irrelevant to practical research or as settled matters requiring no further examination, and thus leaving them implicit in research practice. This book argues instead that ontological awareness is an essential prerequiset for sophisticated social research, and that it enables both critical evaluation of existing studies and informed development and methods choice of new research.

The chapters that follow will develop systematic frameworks for ontological analysis through careful examination of four fundamental debates that shape how researchers approach social phenomena:

- the fundamental units of social analysis (individuals and groups),

- how social arrangements relate to human action (structure and agency),

- what drives social reality (material and ideational factors),

- and whether explanations transcend contexts or remain historically specific (transcendence and immanence).

These debates emerged not from abstract philosophical speculation but from persistent tensions in how researchers actually investigate social life. Understanding them provides analytical tools for recognizing often-hidden assumptions that shape research practice while clarifying what different philosophical commitments make possible and what they constrain. We will go into each of these debates in more depth in part II, but briefly, note that these are not either or, but rather a spectrum that every researcher unavoidable takes position on, even if implicitly.

Beyond these four debates, this book addresses additional dimensions (time, space, and causality) of social ontology that prove essential for adequate social explanation yet often remain implicit in research practice. Time operates in social life not simply as a backdrop against which events unfold but through complex modes that make the past present within current arrangements in ways that transcend simple before-and-after sequences. Space shapes social processes not merely as a neutral container or context but as an actively produced dimension that both reflects and reproduces social relationships and power arrangements. Similarly,causality in social phenomena cannot be reduced to simple cause-and-effect relationships but operates through mechanisms, tendencies, and emergent properties that require sophisticated analytical frameworks to understand adequately and is incorporated differently by different methods. The last dimension is power, which functions as both force and inertia, and can also be described as the tendency of social arrangements to maintain and reproduce themselves, operating through these temporal, spatial, and causal dimensions in ways that are often invisible but nonetheless profoundly shape social possibilities. The way these issues have been conceptualised and the way our assumptions about them affect our methods choices will be discussed more fully in Chapter 9.

This introductory chapter aims to establish why ontological analysis matters for social research and our methods choices and provides philosophical context showing how fundamental questions about reality and knowledge make ontological awareness necessary. This chapter then introduces the analytical framework which will structure the subsequent analysis while clarifying how ontological awareness enables both more sophisticated engagement with existing research and more informed choices about positioning your own investigations and what that means for your methods. We hope that this foundation prepares you for the more detailed examination of the specific debates and analytical tools developed in the chapters that follow.

## 1.1 What is Ontology and Why Does It Matter for Social Research?

Ontology, derived from the Greek *on* (being) and *logos* (study), addresses fundamental questions about what exists and how different aspects of reality relate to each other. In philosophy more broadly, ontology concerns the nature of being itself: what kinds of entities exist, what properties they possess, and how they interact to constitute the reality we inhabit and attempt to understand. Social ontology specifically addresses questions about the nature of social reality: for example, do individual human beings constitute the fundamental units of social life, or do social groups, institutions, and cultures have properties and causal powers that cannot be reduced to individual characteristics? Are social structures external constraints that determine individual behavior, or do human actions continuously create and recreate social arrangements? Can social phenomena be explained through universal patterns that transcend particular contexts, or is social reality fundamentally context-dependent and historically specific?

These ontological questions matter profoundly for social research because how we answer them, whether explicitly or implicitly, determines what we study, how we design research projects, what methods we employ, what counts as adequate evidence, and how we interpret findings. Every research design embeds ontological commitments, whether researchers recognize them or not. For example, choosing to *survey* individuals about their attitudes and behaviors reflects assumptions that individual characteristics constitute appropriate units of analysis and that aggregating individual responses can generate knowledge about social phenomena. In contrast, choosing to *ethnographically observe* organizational cultures reflects assumptions that collective arrangements have emergent properties requiring analysis at the group level rather than reduction to individual psychology.

The problem is that these ontological commitments often remain implicit, as they are inherited unconsciously from a researcher's disciplinary training, methodological traditions, or theoretical frameworks and are never systematically examined. Consider, for example, how economics as a discipline embeds particular ontological commitments that shape its approach to social phenomena. Economics treats individual economic actors (e.g. consumers, firms, workers) as its fundamental units of analysis, collecting data about individual preferences, choices, and behaviors through surveys, experiments, and market observations. These individual-level data are then aggregated through statistical analysis and mathematical modeling to explain collective economic phenomena like market prices, employment rates, or economic growth. This methodological approach reflects deep ontological assumptions: that autonomous individuals constitute the primary reality that we should investigate, that large-scale economic patterns are the sum of individual decision-making guided by rational self-interest, and that a good explanation will show how individual choices aggregate to produce the observable market outcomes. While economics has developed increasing sophistication in analyzing how institutions, social norms, and psychological factors shape individual behavior, its core analytical framework still maintains individualist ontological commitments about where explanatory power ultimately resides and this is most evident in their choice of methods. Still, we can see that these commitments are not arbitrary methodological preferences but reflect fundamental philosophical assumptions about the nature of economic reality itself (Lawson, 1997).

Let us briefly survey some other disciplines and their most (stereo-)typical methods and assumptions. First, for example, sociology, which has developed a primarily collectivist ontology that treats social structures, cultural patterns, and institutional arrangements as having causal powers independently of individuals. Psychology, in contrast, focuses predominantly on individual-level cognitive and behavioral mechanisms while treating social contexts as external influences. Seated somewhere in between, political science exhibits ongoing tensions between individualist approaches examining for example voting behavior and public opinion, while also considering collectivist approaches analyzing institutional arrangements and power structures. These disciplinary differences reflect deep ontological commitments about where causal power resides in social reality and what kinds of entities and processes require investigation to gain knowledge about the social phenomena of interest (Archer, 1995; Bhaskar, 1979).

Understanding these ontological foundations enables two essential practices that provide researchers with a grasp of others and their own positions. The first is *ontological analysis*, which provides critical tools for reading existing research and revealing often-hidden philosophical commitments that shape how studies are designed and interpreted. When research concludes that educational inequality results from individual motivation and family background, ontological analysis reveals multiple ontological commitments operating simultaneously: assumptions about individuals as fundamental units rather than institutional arrangements (individuals-groups debate), emphasis on individual agency and choice rather than structural constraints (structure-agency debate), focus on measurable achievement factors rather than cultural meanings of education (material-ideational debate), and a search for universal predictors rather than context-specific mechanisms (transcendence-immanence debate). This recognition does not automatically invalidate the research but clarifies its theoretical limitations and blind spots.

The second practice, *ontological situating*, enables informed choices about positioning your own research by clarifying what different philosophical commitments you take and what they will allow you to see, versus what they can not tell you anything about. Thus, as previously mentioned, rather than simply adopting positions from disciplinary training, you can actively, and deliberately choose approaches and thus methods that align with your research questions and phenomena under investigation. Choices relating to your positioning operate across the four debates simultaneously - but more on this in Part II. Once you are aware of your own ideas of what social reality is like (by positioning yourself along the four debates), you will understand why some phenomena are more interesting to you, and how you can specify them to become a clear research object. Your position and (ontologically specified) object, will then clarify how to study it based on the properties it has that make it researchable. Based on your specification, you can then derive the most suitable methods for gathering data and analysing it. Your personal ontological situating will then also indicate what your findings can be interpreted and what it can actually speak to. But, centrally, all of these choices should be grounded in a clear, systematic analysis and understanding of whether particular methods can adequately account for patterns you aim to explain (Sayer, 2000). Now this may feel like an insurmountable task, but with this book we hope to break down the process into digestible sections in an attempt to support you through the research project. The remainder of this chapter establishes philosophical foundations necessary for this analytical work, showing how fundamental questions about reality and knowledge contribute to contemporary debates that show that ontological awareness is essential for social research.

## 1.2 The Empiricist Challenge and the Kantian Response: Why Ontology Became Necessary

To understand why ontological analysis matters for contemporary social research, we must examine a crucial philosophical development that transformed how we think about the relationship between reality and knowledge. This development begins with empiricism's challenge to whether we can have genuine knowledge of reality beyond immediate experience. It then continues through Kant's attempt to rescue the possibility of knowledge while fundamentally reconceiving its nature, and culminates in contemporary debates about if, and if so, how reality exists independently of our theories and methods for studying it. Understanding this philosophical trajectory clarifies why researchers cannot simply adopt methods that "work" without examining deeper ontological assumptions. The question of what exists and how we can know it became unavoidable once philosophers recognized that the relationship between reality and knowledge is more complex than it initially appears.

The so-called empiricist tradition, developed most systematically by British philosophers in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, established that, simply put, all knowledge derives from sensory experience rather than from innate ideas or pure reason. John Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689) argued that the mind begins as a blank slate (*tabula rasa*) and acquires all knowledge through experience, either through sensation (perception of external objects) or reflection (awareness of mental operations). This empiricist epistemology had profound appeal because it grounded knowledge in something concrete and verifiable (our actual experiences) rather than in abstract speculation or appeals to authority. Locke distinguished between primary qualities (properties that objects really possess, such as extension, motion, and solidity) and secondary qualities (properties that exist only in our perceptual experience, such as colors, sounds, and tastes), but this distinction itself raised troubling questions. If some aspects of our experience reflect real properties of objects while others reflect only how objects affect our senses, how can we determine which is which? More fundamentally, how can we verify that our ideas correspond to real objects existing independently of our minds? (Locke, 1689; Ayers, 1991).

The empiricist epistemology articulated by Locke did not develop in a philosophical vacuum but was developed in the time of the scientific revolution that had reshaped European intellectual life across the seventeenth century. The Newtonian mechanics that stood as the crowning achievement of that revolution presupposed a specific and consequential ontology: the natural world was composed of discrete, separable particles, each possessing determinate properties that could be measured and described independently of their relations to other particles, and whose interactions could be captured through universal laws of motion. This demonstrated the atomist ontology, which includes the assumption that complex wholes are nothing more than the sum of their constituent parts, and that adequate explanation therefore consists in decomposing phenomena into their elementary units. This view seemed to receive spectacular empirical vindication in Newton's capacity to predict planetary motion, tidal patterns, and the trajectories of projectiles from a small set of precisely stated laws. What atomism displaced was a broadly holist understanding of nature, rooted in Aristotelian and scholastic thought, according to which particular things could only be understood through their participation in larger wholes, their place within teleological orders, and the essences or forms that constituted their nature. On this view, to explain something was not to decompose it into parts but to locate it within the structured whole to which it belonged (Aristotle, *Physics*; Toulmin, 1990). Later, the seductiveness of the atomist model for the nascent social thinkers and early researchers was considerable: if natural philosophy had achieved such explanatory power by treating the world as composed of discrete, law-governed units, perhaps the social world could be understood by the same method, although in this case the particles of classical mechanics would be substituted by individual human beings, with their measurable attitudes, preferences, and behaviors. The atomism-holism debate in the more recent social research, so the question of whether individuals constitute self-sufficient explanatory units or whether social wholes possess properties and causal powers irreducible to those of their parts, originates precisely in this intellectual inheritance (Bhaskar, 1979; Sayer, 2000; Lawson, 1997). This example shows that the way we do science is a debate about ontology, not merely about analytical convenience, and the stakes become fully visible once one recognizes that importing the atomist framework from natural philosophy into social inquiry was a philosophical choice with far-reaching consequences for what social research could and could not see. The causal connections that Newtonian atomism required, the necessary links between discrete units that made prediction and explanation possible, were precisely what Hume demonstrated we could never observe.

David Hume's philosophical work in the mid-eighteenth century subjected these empiricist assumptions to critique that revealed fundamental problems and threatened the possibility of genuine knowledge. In his *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739-1740) and *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748), Hume demonstrated that if we take empiricism seriously, if all knowledge truly derives from experience, then we cannot justify many beliefs that seem essential for scientific investigation. Most famously, Hume argued that we never observe causal connections between events. When we see one billiard ball strike another and the second ball move, we observe spatial contiguity and temporal succession, but we never observe the causal power by which the first ball makes the second move. We simply observe that events of one type regularly follow events of another type, what Hume called "constant conjunction." Hume argued that our belief in causation reflects psychological habit developed through repeated observations of regular successions, not rational inference from experience or direct observation of necessary connections. This means we cannot know that observed causal regularities will continue to hold in future cases. We simply expect them to continue based on past experience, but this expectation cannot actually be rationally justified (Hume, 1739-1740, 1748; Stroud, 1977).

Thus, Hume's skeptical conclusions threatened the foundations of empiricist epistemology by showing that if the empiricist tradition of thought is correct that all knowledge derives from experience, but if experience cannot justify these fundamental beliefs, then perhaps genuine knowledge is impossible. This skeptical challenge had profound implications for how we think about scientific investigation. If we cannot know that observed causal regularities will continue to hold, how can science make predictions or provide reliable guidance for action? If we cannot justify inductive inferences from observed cases to general laws, how can science develop general theories? These questions made clear that the empiricist program, despite its initial appeal in grounding knowledge in concrete experience, faced fundamental philosophical problems (Stroud, 1977; Fogelin, 1985).

Let us move to a thinker who famously responded to this crisis-inducing critique: Immanuel Kant's philosophy, developed across his three Critiques (1781-1790), attempted to rescue the possibility of genuine knowledge from Hume's skeptical conclusions while fundamentally transforming how we understand the relationship between mind and reality. Kant's famous statement that Hume "awoke me from my dogmatic slumber" indicates how seriously he took the skeptical challenge. Kant agreed with Hume that experience alone cannot justify knowledge of causation, induction, or external objects, but he rejected Hume's conclusion that such knowledge is impossible. Instead, Kant proposed a revolutionary solution: the mind actively structures experience through categories that are not derived from experience but constitute necessary conditions for any possible experience. Space and time are not properties of things as they exist in themselves but rather forms of intuition: ways the mind necessarily organizes sensory input to make experience possible. Causation is not observed in experience but is a category the understanding necessarily imposes on experience to make it intelligible. The unity of the self is not observed through introspection but is a transcendental condition, something that must be presupposed as necessary for having any unified experience at all (Kant, 1781; Guyer, 1992).

This "Copernican revolution" in philosophy, so called because Kant proposed that objects must conform to our cognitive faculties rather than our cognitive faculties conforming to objects, had profound and complex implications for how we understand the possibility of knowledge. On the one hand, Kant argued that we can have genuine, objective knowledge of phenomena (things as they appear to us), structured by our mental categories. Mathematical knowledge is possible because space and time are forms of intuition that necessarily structure all possible experience. Scientific knowledge is possible because categories like causation necessarily structure how we experience the world. These forms and categories apply necessarily to all possible experience, which is why mathematical and scientific laws have universal validity (Kant, 1781; Allison, 2004).

However, Kant's solution came with a profound limitation: we can have knowledge only of phenomena (reality as it appears through our conceptual structures) and never of *noumena* (things as they exist in themselves, independent of our minds). We cannot know reality as it is in itself because all knowledge requires the mind's active structuring through categories of understanding. The world we experience and investigate scientifically is necessarily shaped by the forms and categories through which the mind makes experience possible. This means that our knowledge always reflects both features of *external reality* (which provides the sensory content that the mind organizes) and features of *the mind itself* (the forms and categories through which organization occurs). We can never separate these contributions to determine what belongs to reality itself versus what we contribute through our cognitive apparatus (Kant, 1781; Gardner, 1999).

Kant's distinction between phenomena and noumena generated ongoing philosophical debates with profound implications for social research methodology. If we can know only phenomena and never noumena then perhaps reality itself is fundamentally unknowable beyond how it appears through our categories. Different philosophical traditions drew different implications from this Kantian framework. Some concluded that noumena are irrelevant to knowledge and should be eliminated from philosophy, leading toward idealist positions that identify reality with phenomenal experience or with the structures through which mind constitutes experience. Others maintained that noumena must exist even if unknowable, preserving realist commitments to mind-independent reality while acknowledging fundamental limits to human knowledge. Still others argued that Kant's sharp distinction between phenomena and noumena is untenable and should be replaced with more nuanced accounts of how knowledge relates to reality (Allison, 2004; Guyer, 1992).

These competing interpretations of Kant generated fundamental questions that continue shaping contemporary methodological debates in social research. If all knowledge is necessarily structured by conceptual frameworks, categories of understanding, or cultural perspectives, can we ever know social reality as it actually is? Do our theories and methods simply impose structures on social experience rather than discovering structures that exist independently? Are different theoretical frameworks or research paradigms incommensurable, so fundamentally different that they cannot be compared or integrated, because they structure experience in incompatible ways? Can research make progress toward better understanding reality, or does "progress" simply mean developing more elaborate or useful ways of organizing experience without actually approaching truth about mind-independent reality? These questions, which trace back to Kant's critical philosophy and attempts to respond to Hume's skepticism, make clear why contemporary social researchers cannot avoid ontological questions. The relationship between our research frameworks and social reality itself has become philosophically problematic in ways that cannot be resolved simply by collecting more data or refining methodological techniques (Bhaskar, 1975, 1979).

To summarize, the implications of this philosophical trajectory for social research are profound. The empiricist tradition established that reliable knowledge must be grounded in systematic observation and experience rather than in speculation or authority. This remains an essential insight that shapes scientific methodology. However, Hume's skeptical critique revealed that empiricism alone cannot justify the kinds of inferences about causation, general laws, and unobserved entities that scientific investigation requires. Kant's attempt to rescue the possibility of knowledge by arguing that the mind actively structures experience provided a solution to Hume's skepticism, but at the cost of making all knowledge dependent on our conceptual frameworks in ways that raise questions about whether we can ever know reality as it exists independently of these frameworks. This means that contemporary researchers cannot simply rely on empiricist methodology without addressing deeper questions about what exists and how our methods relate to reality. The remainder of this chapter examines how critical realism responds to these philosophical challenges in ways that enable sophisticated ontological analysis while avoiding both naive empiricism and radical skepticism about the possibility of knowing reality.

## 1.3 Realism, Idealism, and Ontology

The philosophical developments traced in the previous section illustrate fundamental questions about the relationship between reality and knowledge that will be helpful to understand why ontological analysis matters for social research. While this project aims to be open to multiple philosophical perspectives, there are places where, at least for now, our grounding philosophy, critical realism, must be drawn on more explicitly if a social science grounded in ontology is to be possible. Key aspects of critical realism will be introduced as they become necessary throughout the textbook. We begin with the question of how reality relates to knowledge, which is answered through Roy Bhaskar's helpful distinction between the intransitive and transitive dimensions of science (Bhaskar, 1975). The intransitive dimension refers to the objects of scientific investigation: the real structures, mechanisms, and processes that exist and operate independently of our knowledge of them. Social institutions, cultural patterns, economic relationships, and power dynamics constitute intransitive objects of social research; they exist and have causal effects whether or not researchers study them accurately or at all. The transitive dimension refers to our changing theories, concepts, and knowledge claims about these intransitive objects: the intellectual frameworks through which we attempt to understand reality. Research paradigms, theoretical traditions, and methodological approaches constitute the transitive dimension; they represent our current best understanding but can be revised, improved, or replaced as knowledge changes.

This distinction proves crucial for understanding the ontological debates examined in this book. The debates about individuals versus groups, structure versus agency, material versus ideational factors, and transcendence versus immanence all concern claims to the intransitive dimension. However, because the transitive dimension can be mis-specified or incomplete (from the perspective of the transitive one), we work with these key ontological debates as analytical frameworks that shape how we currently specify and investigate social phenomena. These debates are not arbitrary classifications but systematic attempts to identify fundamental features of social reality that require ongoing theoretical refinement as understanding develops. Recognizing the distinction between intransitive objects and transitive knowledge helps researchers maintain that social reality has objective features while acknowledging that our understanding of those features remains fallible and subject to revision. This framework enables sophisticated ontological analysis while avoiding both naive empiricism, which reduces reality to observable patterns, and radical skepticism, which denies the possibility of knowing reality beyond our theoretical frameworks (Bhaskar, 1979; Sayer, 2000).

Furthermore, this distinction helps clarify some other debates about objectivity in social research as well. Critical realism distinguishes between ontological realism (the claim that reality exists independently of our knowledge), epistemological relativism (the acknowledgment that all knowledge is socially produced and historically specific), and judgmental rationality (the claim that we can nevertheless evaluate competing theories based on their adequacy in explaining reality)(Bhaskar, 1979; Archer et al., 1998). In other words this means that we can state that reality exists independently, while also acknowledging that all our theories about reality are fallible and culturally influenced, and we can rationally compare theories based on their explanatory power, predictive success, and ability to guide effective intervention.

So, based on this theoretical framework, if reality includes structures, mechanisms, and causal powers that cannot be directly observed but nonetheless operate to generate social phenomena, then adequate research requires theoretical frameworks, and corresponding methods that can provide evidence for their operation through their effects, that can deal with these unobservables. The fact that some entities are unobservable does not make them less real or less important for explanation. Market mechanisms, institutional structures, cultural meanings, and power relations all operate through partially unobservable processes that nonetheless have real causal effects with observable outcomes. Avoiding the epistemic fallacy (see Chapter 2) means recognizing that what exists is not limited to what can be directly observed, measured, or experienced, and that adequate social explanation requires studying the underlying structures and mechanisms that generate observable patterns (Sayer, 2000; Danermark et al., 2002).

## 1.4 Quantification, Objectivity, and the Subject/Object Distinction: Contemporary Manifestations and their Problems

A persistent confusion in methodological discourse conflates the quantitative/qualitative distinction with the opposition between objective and subjective knowledge, treating quantitative methods as inherently more objective while dismissing qualitative approaches as inevitably subjective. This conflation must be directly addressed, as it generates false hierarchies that don't help clarifying what different research projects actually need for good research.

First of all, quantification is fundamentally speaking a technical procedure for numerical representation and has no inherent, nor necessary relationship to objectivity. Objectivity, in terms of science and knowledge, concerns whether knowledge corresponds to what actually exists and operates in social reality independently of the researcher’s preferences or theoretical commitments (Sayer, 2000). These two concepts address different questions. Quantitative analysis can just as easily fail to achieve objectivity if it uses arbitrary variable operationalization, unjustified causal assumptions, or measurement instruments that do not track the phenomena they purport to capture. In contrast, qualitative analysis can achieve high objectivity by accurately capturing how mechanisms actually operate, tracing the specific processes through which structural conditions generate outcomes, or identifying the cultural frameworks through which actors constitute social arrangements. Whether numerical or non-numerical representation is appropriate depends on whether the phenomena under investigation possess *properties that make quantification a valid representational strategy* and whether the analytical questions require the kind of leverage that statistical analysis provides. Hereby we can dispell the myth that quantitative methods equal objectivity.

But let us go a bit deeper; the distinction between subjective and objective operates at two analytically distinct levels. At the ontological level (so what things *are*), the distinction concerns what exists independently of any individual consciousness and what depends on conscious experience. It is also important to note that the idea of objectivity in Western science over the last decades if not centuries has imposed a very particular, quantified, empirical kind of science - this is not what mean. Indeed, the way we would like to suggest to think about objectivity here as the kinds of things that are mind-independent, which means they can be accessed in many different ways. Positing this kind of objectivity actually enables the possibility of other kinds of knowledge, from different groups and perspectives, thus validating the relevance of their lived experiences and (non-academic) knowledge.

Now, let us consider what would be included in this (ontological) conception of objectivity and subjectivity. For example, material conditions, structural arrangements, and institutional rules would be considered, for most positions within the debates, ontologically "objective": they exist and exert causal pressure regardless of whether any particular individual is aware of them. On the other hand, meanings, interpretive frameworks, and normative commitments are ontologically "subjective" in the sense that they depend on the intentional activity of conscious actors, though they may acquire an intersubjective and quasi-objective character when collectively shared and institutionally reproduced.

At the epistemological level (so the level of *how can we know*?), the distinction of the objective and subjective indicates whether knowledge corresponds to how things actually exist and operate (objective), or whether it reflects the researcher’s prior commitments, methodological habits, or theoretical preferences rather than the phenomena themselves (subjective). A researcher can (hypothetically speaking) produce epistemologically objective knowledge about ontologically subjective phenomena, by for example accurately reconstructing how actors experience meanings or institutional pressures, just as another can produce epistemologically subjective knowledge about ontologically objective phenomena by selecting evidence that confirms theoretical expectations. Do note, that the fully epistemologically objective approach would be extremely difficult if not impossible, as we are humans and thus not omniscient and that will probably always hold some unacknowledged assumptions that will make our knowledge partial and fallible. However, the standard of objectivity also depends on the researcher’s position on the transcendence/immanence debate: a transcendent position assesses objectivity through correspondence of causal claims to properties that hold across different cases, while an immanent position assesses it through accuracy of interconnected reconstruction - but more on this in Chapter 8. What stays the same is the requirement that claims correspond to something in social reality rather than to the researcher’s prior commitments.

Beyond these two levels lies yet another, a third, distinction that is directly relevant for research design: the distinction between the subject and the object of research. The subject of any kind of research is the researcher as knowing agent, with particular theoretical commitments, positional situatedness, and interpretive capacities. The object of research is whatever aspect or phenomenon of social reality the investigation aims to understand: the mechanisms, entities, processes, or configurations that exist independently of the investigation directed at them. The relationship between subject and object of research is not fixed but is itself shaped by the researcher’s combined ontological position. A researcher who holds that mechanisms operate in a domain entirely independent of any observer’s awareness treats the object of research as fully intransitive: it exists and operates the same way whether or not the research is conducted, and the task is to develop knowledge adequate to what is already there. A researcher who holds that the act of investigation, the concepts brought to bear, the questions asked, and the categories through which findings are organised, partly constitutes what is available to be known treats the relationship between subject and object as more complex: the object is not simply given prior to investigation but is partly shaped by the investigative practices through which it is accessed.[^2]

However, this does not necessarily collapse into the claim that the object of research is whatever the researcher makes of it. The intransitive dimension, what exists and operates independently of knowledge of it, is the referent that gives research its claim to produce genuine knowledge rather than merely organised opinion (Bhaskar, 1979). The transitive dimension, the concepts, methods, and theoretical frameworks through which that reality is, is investigated, shapes what aspects of the intransitive (so the mind-independent) dimension become visible and what remains outside the frame of investigation. A researcher who approaches educational inequality with concepts adequate only to individual-level variation will not find emergent collective properties even if those properties are real and causally efficacious. These will not be evident, not because they do not exist, but because the investigative framework cannot grasp them. The distinction between subject and object of research therefore carries a reflexive demand: the researcher must be aware of not only what is being investigated, but also how the conceptual and methodological apparatus they want to use shapes what the investigation can and cannot see and study. Making the theoretical framework (and thus its ontological assumptions) explicit is precisely the practice through which this reflexive demand is addressed, since an explicit framework can be evaluated for its adequacy to the object rather than simply applied as if its adequacy were guaranteed.

## 1.5 How This Leads to the Quantitative/Qualitative Divide and the Problem with Methods in Themselves

To summarize, the confusions addressed in the preceding section are not merely conceptual errors correctable by greater precision. They are reproduced by structural features of how the social sciences are organised and how methods are taught. When quantification is systematically associated with objectivity in disciplinary training and publication norms, the association becomes self-reinforcing: researchers who want their work recognised as rigorous select quantitative methods not because those methods are adequate to their research objects but because the association with objectivity grants legitimacy. This generates a hierarchy among methods that is unresponsive to the ontological requirements of actual research problems, and it is this hierarchy that produces the quantitative/qualitative divide in its contemporary institutionalised form.

As previously discussed, the divide is not, at its source, a disagreement about methods. It is an expression of deeper disagreements about what social reality is, how it is constituted, and what kinds of access are possible to it. Treating the divide as primarily a methodological dispute misses this. It encourages researchers to resolve the tension by combining quantitative and qualitative techniques, which is does not resolve the fundamental issue as long as the methods choices are not combined with an explicitly worked-out ontological position that determines what each technique is doing and why. After all, the method specifies what can be seen, what counts as evidence, and what explanatory forms are available. When the method is chosen before the ontological position is explicit, it effectively settles the ontological question in advance: the findings will be shaped by what the method can reach, and what it cannot conceptualize or fit into a relevant unit of analysis will remain invisible. The researcher who equates rigour with the application of a specific method, regardless of whether that method is adequate to the mechanisms or configurations under investigation, has made an implicit ontological commitment without subjecting it to scrutiny. Making that commitment explicit is what enables the researcher to evaluate whether the method is appropriate and helps them identify the limits of what the findings can establish. This is this book is designed for. In Chapter 2 we begin that task by setting out the conceptual architecture on which everything else rests: the relationships between ontology, epistemology, methodology, and method.
